Commercial Cleaning Proposals and Contracts: How to Win Office and Property Manager Accounts

By Nakul Chandra, Founder and Lead Strategist · November 21, 2026 · 20 min read

Commercial cleaning looks simple from the outside. Find an office, quote a price, start cleaning. In practice, the companies that grow fastest are not the ones with the best mops. They are the ones who are best at the part before the cleaning: finding the right buyers, walking the building, pricing accurately, writing a proposal that makes the decision easy and signing a contract that protects both sides.

A strong commercial account can be worth tens of thousands of dollars over its life. A badly priced one can cost you money every single night. The difference is almost always in how the deal was built.

This guide walks through that process from first contact to signed agreement, written for commercial and janitorial cleaning companies in the United States. We use examples from our four focus states: Florida, Michigan, Illinois and Texas. If you want the online side first, our guide to winning commercial cleaning contracts online covers websites, SEO and lead generation. This article picks up at the moment someone says, "Can you send us a quote?"

The short version: Commercial buyers choose the company that feels safest and easiest, not always the cheapest. Win by qualifying the lead, doing a proper walk-through, pricing from your real costs, sending a proposal that answers the buyer's worries in plain language, and offering a contract with clear scope, insurance and quality terms. Then follow up until you get a decision.

Understand who you are selling to

Commercial buyers are not all the same. Knowing who you are dealing with changes what you say and how you price.

Office managers and small business owners

They want a reliable crew, a fair price and no headaches. They often make the decision alone, within days. They notice small things: whether you replied quickly, whether you arrived on time to the walk-through, whether your quote was easy to read.

Property managers and building managers

They oversee one or many buildings and answer to owners. They need vendors who are insured, consistent and responsive. Their decisions often involve a vendor application, a certificate of insurance and a trial period. One signed property manager can lead to multiple buildings.

Facility managers at larger organizations

Medical offices, schools, manufacturing sites and corporate campuses have a facility manager who evaluates multiple bids, often with a formal request for proposal. They want proof of experience, safety practices, references and sometimes compliance with specific standards.

Procurement teams and bid processes

Larger organizations and public entities use procurement departments and formal bids. These have strict forms, deadlines and scoring. They are slower, but the contracts are larger and often longer.

What all of them have in common

They are worried about the same things: that the cleaners will not show up, that the work will be inconsistent, that something will be stolen or damaged, that the price will creep up, and that switching vendors again will be painful. A great proposal addresses each of those worries directly.

Qualify before you quote

Not every lead is worth a walk-through. Qualifying saves hours and stops you from taking jobs that will lose money.

Ask these questions on the first call or form:

  • What type of building is it (office, medical, retail, industrial, school, multi-tenant)?
  • Approximately how many square feet, and how many floors?
  • How many restrooms, break rooms and conference rooms?
  • What is the floor mix: carpet, vinyl, tile, hardwood, concrete?
  • How often do they want service, and at what hours?
  • Who is the current cleaner, and why are they looking at change?
  • Who makes the decision, and when do they want to start?
  • Are there special requirements such as medical-grade disinfection, secure areas or sign-in procedures?
  • Do they have a budget range in mind?

A buyer who cannot say who decides, or who wants an exact price over the phone with no visit, is not ready. Politely explain that an accurate quote needs a walk-through and offer a time.

The walk-through: where deals are won

A proper walk-through does three things. It lets you measure and price accurately, it shows the buyer you are professional and it gives you a chance to learn what they truly care about.

Before you go

  • Confirm the time and the person you are meeting
  • Research the company and the building
  • Bring a tablet or a printed checklist, a measuring wheel or laser measure, a camera and business cards
  • Dress to match the client: professional, clean, branded if you have uniforms

During the walk-through

  • Measure and count. Square footage by floor type, number of restrooms and fixtures, offices, desks, trash bins, windows, entrances, kitchens, elevators and stairs.
  • Note conditions. Heavy traffic areas, stained carpets, hard floors that need finishing, areas with specialized needs.
  • Ask about pain points. "What frustrates you most about the current cleaning?" The answer tells you what to emphasize.
  • Learn the logistics. Access, alarm codes, key policy, supplies storage, parking, loading docks and trash disposal.
  • Take photos (with permission) to help your pricing and to document pre-existing damage.
  • Observe the people. How many staff, what hours, whether the space is used on weekends.

After the walk-through

Send a thank you within a few hours and promise the proposal by a specific date. Then keep your promise. Many companies lose the job at this step simply by being slow.

How to price a commercial cleaning bid

Pricing is where cleaning companies most often go wrong. Quote too low and you lose money every night. Quote too high with no explanation and you lose the bid. The aim is a price that covers every cost, includes a profit and can be explained in one paragraph.

Three common pricing methods

MethodHow it worksStrengthsWatch out for
------------
Per square footPrice per sq ft per month or per visitQuick, easy to compareIgnores building complexity
HourlyEstimated hours times a billing rateReflects labor directlyBuyers may question hours
Per task or per roomPrice per restroom, office, etc.Good for add-onsCan become complicated

Many experienced operators use the hourly method internally to check any per square foot number, because labor time is the real cost driver.

Build the number from labor up

Follow this process:

  1. Estimate production time. Using your own production rates, estimate hours per visit for each area: restrooms, offices, open workspace, break rooms, entry and floors. Your own tracked data from past jobs is the most reliable source.
  2. Add travel and setup time for the crew.
  3. Multiply by your fully loaded labor cost. That is wages plus payroll taxes, workers' compensation, insurance share and benefits where offered.
  4. Add supplies and consumables, such as chemicals, liners, paper products if you supply them and equipment wear.
  5. Add overhead, such as management, vehicles, software, training, uniforms and administration.
  6. Add profit. Decide your target margin and apply it.
  7. Convert to the monthly price by multiplying by the number of visits.

A worked example, with made-up numbers for illustration only

Imagine a 6,000 square foot office, cleaned five nights a week. Suppose your production estimate is 3 labor hours per visit. If your fully loaded cost is a certain amount per hour, you multiply that by 3 for labor. You add a small amount per visit for supplies, then overhead as a percentage, then your profit margin. Multiply by roughly 21 to 22 visits a month and you get the monthly price. Then divide by square footage to get the per square foot figure and compare it to your market. If it looks very different from competitors, check your production rates before you change the price.

We deliberately do not give dollar figures here, because wages, insurance and market rates differ so much between Houston, Chicago, Miami and Detroit. Build your own sheet with your own costs, and update it every year.

Pricing mistakes to avoid

  • Copying a competitor's number without knowing their costs
  • Forgetting travel time, supervision and supply costs
  • Ignoring the cost of turnover and training for hourly staff
  • Quoting a flat price for an unclear scope
  • Giving a deep discount to win, with no plan to raise the price later
  • Not including a clause for annual increases or scope changes

How to present the price

Give a single monthly price with a short breakdown of what is included. Offer a second option when it helps: for example, a core package and an enhanced package with extra tasks such as high dusting or carpet extraction. Options let the buyer choose a level rather than a yes or no, and many pick the middle one.

What a winning proposal looks like

A proposal is not a price list. It is a sales document that should leave the buyer feeling that you understood their building, their worries and their goals. It should be easy to read in five minutes by someone who is busy.

The structure that works

  1. Cover page. Your name, logo, the client's name and building, the date and the contact person.
  2. A short summary. One paragraph repeating what the client told you they need and what you will deliver. This shows you listened.
  3. Scope of work. A clear list of tasks by area and frequency. Use a table, not a wall of text.
  4. Service schedule. Days, hours and crew size.
  5. Your approach and quality control. How you train, supervise and inspect. Mention checklists, supervisor visits and a feedback channel.
  6. Safety, insurance and compliance. Your coverage, background checks and relevant training. Attach the certificate of insurance.
  7. References and proof. Two or three similar clients with permission to be contacted, plus reviews and a short case example.
  8. Pricing. The monthly price, what it includes, add-on prices and payment terms.
  9. Terms summary. Term length, notice period, price adjustment policy and start date.
  10. Next steps. A clear action, such as "Sign here to start on [date]" and a call to schedule a kickoff.

Write for the reader

  • Use plain language and short paragraphs
  • Use the client's own words about their needs
  • Lead with benefits: consistency, communication, accountability
  • Keep it to three to six pages for a small or mid-size account
  • Include photos of your team and sample inspection forms

Adjust this to the building, and be explicit about what is not included so there are no surprises.

Show your quality system

Buyers fear inconsistency more than anything. Show them how you prevent it:

  • A written checklist for each area
  • A supervisor inspection schedule, such as weekly for the first month and monthly after
  • A simple way for the client to report an issue and a promised response time
  • A defined process for corrections, such as a re-clean within a set number of hours
  • Cross-trained staff to cover sick days

Include a trial or a clean start offer

Offering a 30 or 60 day trial with a short notice period lowers risk for the buyer. A clean start, such as a one-time initial deep clean, helps you establish a high standard on day one and justifies a setup fee.

Contracts: the terms that protect you and reassure the buyer

A clear agreement prevents arguments later. We are not lawyers, so have an attorney licensed in your state review your template. These are the topics to cover.

Scope and standards

  • The scope of work attached as a schedule
  • Service days and times
  • What is excluded and how extra work is quoted
  • A quality standard and the inspection process

Term, renewal and termination

  • Start date and initial term, often 6 to 12 months
  • Automatic renewal terms, if any
  • Notice for termination by either side, commonly 30 days
  • Termination for cause, such as repeated missed service or non-payment

Pricing and payment

  • Monthly price and what it covers
  • Invoice date and payment terms, such as net 15 or net 30
  • Late fees and the right to suspend service for non-payment
  • Annual price adjustment, either a fixed percentage or tied to a published index
  • How scope changes are priced

Insurance and liability

  • Your general liability coverage and limits
  • Workers' compensation as required by your state
  • Bonding or crime coverage if offered
  • Certificate of insurance and additional insured wording where requested
  • A reasonable limit of liability, as your attorney advises
  • How damage claims are reported and resolved

Access, security and confidentiality

  • Key and alarm code control
  • Background checks for staff
  • Confidentiality of the client's information and premises
  • Rules about after-hours entry and sign-in

Staffing and compliance

  • That you are responsible for your own employees, wages and taxes
  • Compliance with employment and safety laws
  • Training in chemical safety and hazard communication
  • Any specialized training for medical, food or other regulated environments

Supplies and equipment

  • Who supplies chemicals, liners, paper products and equipment
  • Storage space and its use
  • Environmental or green product requirements, if any

Non-solicitation

Some contracts include a clause stopping the client from hiring your staff directly. Discuss this with your attorney.

Force majeure and special events

Cover events such as severe weather or building closures and how service and billing are handled. In states like Florida and Texas, storm and hurricane preparation can matter. Michigan and Illinois buyers will think about snow and winter access.

Compliance and credibility items buyers ask for

Even a small office manager will often ask: "Are you insured?" Bigger buyers ask for documents. Have these ready in one folder:

  • Certificate of insurance
  • Business license and registration
  • W-9
  • Workers' compensation proof
  • Safety program and chemical safety training records
  • Background check policy
  • Sample checklists and inspection forms
  • References and case examples
  • Any relevant certifications or training programs you hold

Do not claim a certification you do not have. If you are working toward one, say so honestly. For specialized settings such as medical offices or schools, check the specific requirements with the client and with qualified professionals.

Find and win the right buyers

Strong proposals need a steady flow of qualified leads. There are four main sources, and the best companies use all of them.

1. Your website and Google presence

Commercial buyers search "commercial cleaning [city]", "janitorial services near me" and "office cleaning [city]." If your website does not have dedicated pages for commercial cleaning, medical office cleaning and property management cleaning, you will miss those searches. A commercial buyer also checks whether the site looks like a company that can handle a contract. Our web design for cleaning companies team builds commercial pages with proof, insurance statements, an industries served section and a quote form that qualifies leads.

2. Direct outreach

Make a target list of 100 buildings and organizations in your service area. Use public information about local office parks, medical practices, property managers and professional firms. Send a short, personalized email, follow up by phone and drop by with a one-page brochure when appropriate. Be respectful, and always follow email laws, which require accurate sender details and an opt-out option.

3. Referral partners

Property managers, commercial real estate agents, facilities contractors, HVAC companies, security firms and general contractors all meet building owners and managers. Offer a simple referral arrangement and make it easy for them to recommend you.

4. Bids and vendor lists

Register on local government, school and hospital vendor lists where relevant. Join the chamber of commerce and local property management associations. Many contracts never go public, and being on an approved vendor list gets you invited.

Scripts for the commercial sales process

Cold email to an office manager

Subject: Cleaning for [Company Name] at [Address]

"Hi [Name], I run [Company], a commercial cleaning team serving offices around [neighborhood or city]. We are fully insured, our crews are background checked and every account gets a weekly supervisor inspection. If the current cleaning is meeting your needs, great. If you ever want a second quote, I would be happy to do a short walk-through at no charge. Would next Tuesday or Wednesday work for a 20 minute visit?"

Phone script for a property manager

"Hi [Name], this is [Your name] from [Company]. We provide commercial cleaning for office and multi-tenant buildings in [city]. I wanted to ask who manages your vendor relationships and whether you ever look for a backup or replacement cleaner. [Listen.] Could I send over our insurance certificate and a one-page overview, and perhaps visit one of your buildings for a quote?"

Walk-through opening

"Thanks for meeting me. Before I measure, can you tell me what is working and what is frustrating about your cleaning right now? I want to make sure what I propose solves the real problems, not just the obvious ones."

Proposal delivery email

"Hi [Name], thank you for showing me [Building] today. Attached is our proposal. In short: [two sentence summary of scope and price]. I included our insurance certificate and two references who are happy to speak with you. If it suits you, I can schedule a kickoff walk-through for [date] so we can start on [date]. I will call you Thursday morning to answer any questions."

Follow-up after no response

"Hi [Name], I wanted to check in on the proposal for [Building]. If timing has changed or you would like to adjust the scope or price, I am happy to revise it. If you have chosen another provider, no problem at all, and I would appreciate hearing what made the difference so I can improve."

Handling the price objection

When the buyer says "Another company quoted lower," try:

"That is useful to know. Can I ask what their scope includes? I want to be sure we are comparing the same things, such as service days, crew size, supplies and insurance. If the scope is the same, I am happy to look at where we can adjust. If theirs includes less, I can show you what is different."

Do not drop your price immediately. Re-scope, offer a smaller package or explain the value. Cut price only if the numbers still work.

Follow-up: the quiet advantage

Many cleaning companies send a proposal and wait. Buyers are busy, and decisions stall. A simple follow-up routine wins deals others lose.

  • Day 0: Send the proposal and a short summary email.
  • Day 2: A quick check that it arrived and whether there are questions.
  • Day 7: A call or message, offering to adjust scope.
  • Day 14: Share a short case study or review.
  • Day 30: A friendly check-in. Ask if anything changed.
  • Quarterly after that: Keep warm with a useful note, a seasonal service offer or a short check-in.

Track every proposal in a simple sheet: date sent, price, decision-maker, next action and result. Over time you learn your win rate and which lead sources and pricing levels convert.

Onboarding: protect the account from day one

The first month decides whether the account lasts.

  • Kickoff meeting with the client contact to confirm scope, access, alarms and communication.
  • Initial deep clean to set the baseline.
  • Introduce the crew leader and give the client a direct contact number.
  • Inspect daily for the first week and weekly after.
  • Hold a 30 day review and ask what to adjust.
  • Ask for a review or a reference after the first successful month.

Strong onboarding turns a nervous new client into a reference you can use to win the next one.

Growing from one account to many

Once you have a handful of accounts, systems matter more than hustle.

  • Standardize checklists, training and inspection forms.
  • Use scheduling and tracking software to manage crews, routes and time.
  • Create supervisors so you are not the quality control for every building.
  • Track profitability by account. Review each account every quarter and fix or drop the ones that lose money.
  • Raise prices on schedule. Use your annual adjustment clause.
  • Build a sales pipeline with monthly targets for walk-throughs and proposals.

Common mistakes in commercial cleaning bids

  • Quoting without a walk-through. Phone quotes lead to underpricing and disputes.
  • Using only price per square foot. It hides complexity and can sink your margin.
  • Sending a generic proposal. Buyers can tell, and it signals lack of care.
  • No insurance documents ready. Delays kill deals.
  • Vague scope. It invites scope creep and arguments.
  • No price adjustment clause. Costs rise, and you cannot recover them.
  • Forgetting to follow up. Most deals are lost to silence, not to a competitor.
  • Taking every account. Bad fits drain your team and your cash.
  • Weak online presence. If a buyer looks you up and finds nothing, they hesitate.

A 90 day plan to build your commercial pipeline

Days 1 to 15: Foundations

  • Build your cost sheet and production rates
  • Create your proposal template and contract, and have an attorney review it
  • Gather insurance certificates, W-9 and references into one folder
  • Write your standard checklists and inspection form

Days 16 to 45: Visibility

  • Add or improve commercial pages on your website: commercial cleaning, medical office, property management and a page for each priority city
  • Update your Google Business Profile with commercial services and proof
  • Collect reviews from existing commercial clients
  • Build a list of 100 target buildings and organizations

Days 46 to 90: Outreach and conversion

  • Send personalized outreach to 20 targets a week with follow-up calls
  • Book at least 8 to 12 walk-throughs
  • Send every proposal within 48 hours and follow up on the schedule above
  • Track results and adjust pricing and messaging
  • Ask each new client for a reference

At the end of 90 days, you should have a working pipeline, a measured win rate and a clearer sense of your best client type.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my competitor rank higher than me on Google?

Usually they have a stronger Google Business Profile, a separate page for each service and area, more recent reviews, and more local websites linking to them. Their website being nicer is rarely the reason.

Can I pay Google to rank higher in the map results?

No. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. You can run ads, but they appear separately and are marked as ads.

How do I get ChatGPT to recommend my cleaning company?

There is no guaranteed method. The basics that help on Google tend to help here too: a complete Google profile, steady reviews, consistent details across the web and a helpful website with a page for each service and area.

Do I need special code or an llms.txt file for AI search?

Google says you don't need special markup or an llms.txt file to appear in its AI features. Focus on helpful pages and strong basics first.

How long will it take to overtake a competitor?

It depends on how far ahead they are and what is holding you back. Anyone who promises a date is guessing. A realistic goal is to fix the basics in 30 days, then keep improving each month.

Does distance matter, and can I change it?

Yes, Google lists distance as one of three factors. You can't move closer to customers, but you can improve relevance and prominence, which is where most of the gains are.
