Short-Term Rentals

Airbnb and Short-Term Rental Cleaning: How to Win Host and Property Manager Contracts

By Nakul Chandra, Founder and Lead Strategist · November 21, 2026 · 22 min read

The short-term rental market is one of the few corners of the cleaning business where demand shows up every week, all year, tied to a calendar you can see in advance. Every guest checkout creates a job. A host with a busy listing needs a turnover clean after almost every stay, often within a few hours, and needs it done the same way every time.

For a cleaning company, that is the kind of work operators dream about: repeat clients, predictable schedules, and a customer who cares more about reliability than about the lowest price. It is also work that can go wrong quickly if you are not set up for it. A missed turnover means a guest arrives to a dirty unit, a bad review and an angry host.

This guide is the practical playbook for winning that work in the United States. It covers who your buyers are, how to price turnovers, how to find and approach hosts and property managers, the scripts that work, the contract terms that protect you, and the website and Google presence that make hosts trust you before they call. We use examples from our four focus states: Florida, Michigan, Illinois and Texas.

The short version: Short-term rental cleaning is won by being the most reliable and the easiest to work with, not the cheapest. Know your numbers, offer a clear turnover package with photo proof, approach property managers before individual hosts, put your terms in writing, and make it easy for hosts to find, trust and book you online.

Who actually buys short-term rental cleaning

Not every buyer is the same, and your approach should change with each type. There are four main ones.

Individual hosts with one or two units

These are homeowners or investors who list a spare room, a condo or a house. Some live nearby and clean themselves until they burn out. Others are out of town owners who need someone else to handle everything. They care about trust, communication and price. They are the easiest to find and the quickest to decide, but they have the smallest volume and the highest churn when a property sells or they decide to self-manage.

Hosts with portfolios of 3 to 20 units

These operators run rentals as a real business. They think in terms of cost per turnover, time between checkouts and check-ins, and review scores. They often already have a cleaner and will switch only if you are clearly better. They value consistency and clear reporting.

Property management companies

Vacation rental managers handle dozens or hundreds of units for owners. They often have a vendor list and a set process for adding cleaners. One signed property manager can be worth more than fifty individual hosts. They also demand more: insurance, background checks, software compatibility and the ability to scale during peak periods.

Hotels, boutique stays and corporate housing

Boutique hotels, extended stay properties and corporate housing providers have rhythms similar to short-term rentals but often want contracts, formal bidding and detailed checklists. These sit closer to commercial cleaning, and our guide to winning commercial cleaning contracts with proposals and agreements covers the proposal side in detail.

Short-term rental demand follows tourism, events and seasons. A quick map of what we see in the markets we focus on:

  • Florida. Orlando, Miami, Tampa and the Gulf and Atlantic coasts have heavy year-round vacation rental demand, with spikes around school holidays, spring break and snowbird season. Many owners live out of state and rely entirely on local managers and cleaners. See our Orlando, Miami and Tampa pages.
  • Texas. Austin leans on events, festivals and business travel. Houston has corporate stays, medical travelers and event weekends. Dallas Fort Worth has sports and convention traffic. Check our Austin and Houston pages and our Dallas page.
  • Michigan. Lake Michigan shoreline towns and northern lake communities run strong summer seasons and winter ski and snowmobile periods. Detroit and Ann Arbor see event and university-related stays. See Michigan and Detroit.
  • Illinois. Chicago has year-round business and tourism, with strong summer peaks and event weekends. The suburbs see wedding and family-visit demand. See Illinois.

Use that as a starting point, then confirm local demand yourself by looking at active listings in your service area. A quick scan of how many whole-home listings exist near you tells you more than any national report.

Know the job before you price it

A turnover clean is different from a regular house clean. You are resetting the unit for a stranger arriving in a few hours, and the standard is closer to hotel housekeeping than to a weekly maid visit.

What a turnover usually includes

  • Strip and remake beds with fresh linens, check mattress protectors and pillows
  • Clean and sanitize all bathrooms, including grout, mirrors and fixtures
  • Kitchen reset: wipe counters and appliances, clean the inside of the microwave, empty the fridge of leftovers, run or empty the dishwasher
  • Vacuum and mop all floors, including under furniture where reachable
  • Dust surfaces, wipe switches, handles and remote controls
  • Empty all trash and replace liners
  • Restock consumables such as toilet paper, soap, coffee and shampoo, as agreed with the host
  • Check for damage, missing items and anything left behind by guests, and report it with photos
  • Staging: fold towels, set pillows, close curtains, set the thermostat and lock up

What makes it harder than it looks

  • Tight windows. Checkout may be at 11 a.m. and check-in at 3 p.m., leaving four hours including travel and laundry.
  • Variable condition. One guest leaves the place spotless. The next leaves a party's worth of damage.
  • Laundry. Someone has to handle sheets and towels. Decide whether you wash on site, off site or use a linen service.
  • Same-day changes. Hosts change reservations, and you need a clear way to handle last-minute turnovers.
  • Inspection and photos. Hosts want proof the job was done, which means a checklist and time-stamped photos for every unit.

Understanding these details lets you price accurately and speak confidently when a host asks how you handle them.

How to price short-term rental turnovers

Price is where many cleaners lose money on this work. They quote a number that looks fair and then discover the real time cost of laundry, restocking, travel and inspection.

Start with your real costs

Calculate, for a typical one-bedroom and a typical three-bedroom:

  • Average time on site for a standard turnover
  • Number of cleaners needed
  • Labor cost per hour including payroll taxes and workers' compensation where required
  • Travel time and fuel between jobs
  • Supplies per turnover
  • Laundry costs, whether in-house or outsourced
  • Insurance and overhead share
  • A profit margin you can live with

Common pricing models

ModelHow it worksBest for

For many companies, flat rate by size with a clearly written add-on list works best. Hosts like knowing the number in advance, and you avoid disputes about time.

Illustrative ranges, and why you must check your own

Turnover prices vary a great deal by city, unit size, laundry arrangement and season. A small studio in a mid-size market and a four-bedroom beach house in a premium location can differ by several hundred dollars. We will not give a number that looks authoritative but is wrong for your market. Instead, do this:

  • Search the websites of five competitors in your area for turnover pricing
  • Check what hosts say they pay in local host groups and forums
  • Call two property managers and ask what they currently pay per turnover (many will tell you)
  • Compare against your own cost sheet and set a price that covers cost with a healthy margin

Add-ons that raise revenue honestly

  • Deep clean between stays, billed separately, on a schedule such as quarterly
  • Laundry service, on site or off site
  • Restocking with host-supplied or company-supplied consumables
  • Same-day or short-notice turnover surcharge
  • Carpet, upholstery or mattress cleaning
  • Window and patio cleaning
  • Welcome basket setup or special staging
  • Inspection reports with extra photos

Avoid the race to the bottom

Hosts who choose purely on price tend to switch at the first cheaper offer. Hosts who choose on reliability and communication stay for years. Aim for the second group. Your pricing page and your sales conversations should show the value: proof photos, backup staffing, clear communication and insured crews.

Build a short-term rental offer hosts can say yes to

Before you pitch anyone, package your service so it is simple to understand and hard to compare on price alone. A clear offer might look like this.

Your turnover package

  • A defined checklist, shared with the host and adjusted to their unit
  • Photo proof of every room after each clean, uploaded to a shared folder or app
  • A same-day report of damage, missing items and low supplies
  • A guaranteed response time for last-minute changes
  • A backup cleaner plan if someone is sick or unavailable
  • Insurance and background checks stated clearly
  • Simple monthly invoicing or card on file billing

The things hosts care about most

Ask ten hosts what they want from a cleaner, and the same few answers appear:

  • Reliability. Show up on time, every time.
  • Consistency. The unit looks the same after every clean.
  • Communication. Tell me about problems before the guest does.
  • Trust. Do not steal, do not break things, do not disappear.
  • Flexibility. Handle changes in the calendar without a fight.
  • Fair pricing. No surprises on the invoice.

Everything in your offer should be designed around those six.

Where to find short-term rental clients

There are two broad ways to find clients: go where hosts already are, and be easy to find when they search. You need both.

Go where hosts already are

  • Local host groups. Many cities have Facebook groups and online communities for hosts. Be helpful first. Answer questions, share genuine tips and mention your service when it fits. Read the group rules, because many ban direct promotion.
  • Property management companies. Search for vacation rental managers in your area and visit their sites. Many list a vendor application or an email for service providers.
  • Real estate agents and investors. Agents who sell to investors often get asked for cleaner recommendations. A short introduction and a free first clean for their own listing can build a strong referral source.
  • Co-hosting businesses. Some companies manage listings for owners and outsource the cleaning. These are fast routes to volume.
  • Local meetups and investor groups. Real estate investor meetups and STR meetups are full of potential clients.
  • Direct observation. Look at active listings near you. Many list a neighborhood, a review count and a calendar. Reading the reviews shows you where cleanliness complaints appear, and those hosts are your best prospects.
  • Reviews mentioning cleanliness. A host with recent reviews that mention dust, hair or bad smells has a problem you can solve. Approach respectfully and focus on how you help, not on criticizing them.

Be easy to find when hosts search

Hosts search for cleaners just like homeowners do, with phrases such as "Airbnb cleaning service [city]," "vacation rental turnover cleaning [city]" and "short term rental cleaners near me." If your website has no page for this service, you will not appear.

Create a dedicated page for short-term rental turnover cleaning, ideally with city variations for your priority markets. Include your process, a clear service area, a pricing explanation, an FAQ and a visible way to request a quote. That is the same structure we recommend in local SEO for cleaning companies, applied to this specific buyer.

Also check that your Google Business Profile lists short-term rental cleaning as a service and that recent reviews mention turnovers. Reviews that say "they handled our Airbnb turnovers" are gold. When you ask for reviews from hosts, mention that detail kindly.

Outreach scripts that work

Most cold outreach fails because it is generic. The goal is to start a conversation, not to close a sale in the first message. Keep it short, specific and respectful. Below are templates to adapt. Always personalize the first line, and never invent details about the host's property.

Script 1: Message to an individual host (text or direct message)

"Hi [Name], I noticed your [neighborhood] listing has great reviews. I run [Company], a licensed and insured cleaning team that does turnovers for hosts in [city]. We send photos of every room after each clean and flag any damage the same day. If you ever need backup or want to compare, happy to send our checklist. No pressure either way."

Why it works: it is specific, it offers value (checklist), it states the proof (photos, insured) and it asks for nothing big.

Script 2: Email to a vacation rental property manager

Subject: Turnover cleaning support for your [city] properties

"Hi [Name], I am [Your name] with [Company]. We provide insured, background-checked turnover cleaning teams for vacation rentals in [city and areas]. We use a photo-verified checklist, can scale up for holiday weekends and have backup staffing for last-minute calls. I would like to be considered for your vendor list. Could I send our insurance certificate, references and a sample inspection report? Happy to do a free trial turnover on one unit so you can judge our work."

Why it works: it speaks their language (vendor list, scale, backup), shows the documents they will ask for and offers a low risk trial.

Script 3: Follow-up after silence (send 5 to 7 days later)

"Hi [Name], following up on my note about turnover support for your [city] units. If your current team is covering everything well, no problem. If you ever have a cancellation, a peak weekend or an unhappy owner, I would be glad to be your backup. I can start with a single turnover whenever it suits you."

Script 4: Phone call to a property manager

"Hi, this is [Name] with [Company]. I am a local cleaning company that does short-term rental turnovers, and I wanted to ask who handles your vendor list. [Pause.] Great. Could I send them a one-page summary with our insurance and sample photos? We would love to be on your backup list for busy weekends."

Why it works: it asks for the right person and a tiny commitment, which is easier to say yes to.

Script 5: Referral request to a happy host

"Hi [Name], thanks so much for trusting us with your turnovers. If you know another host or manager who needs reliable cleaning, I would really appreciate an introduction. I will make sure they get the same attention you do."

Rules for outreach

  • Personalize every message. Mass copy and paste messages get ignored or reported.
  • Follow platform rules. Do not spam host groups or message guests through platforms.
  • Respect email laws. In the US, commercial emails must include accurate sender information and an easy way to opt out.
  • Never claim insurance, licenses or certifications you do not hold.
  • Offer a trial, not a long contract, as the first step.

The trial turnover: how to turn interest into a contract

A paid or free trial turnover is the strongest closer in this niche. Treat it as an audition.

  • Agree on scope in writing before the day: checklist, laundry, restocking, photo requirements.
  • Arrive early and document the unit's condition on arrival with photos.
  • Do the job to your standard and a little beyond: check light bulbs, wipe the door frames, note any maintenance issues.
  • Send photos and a short report within an hour of finishing.
  • Follow up the next day to ask if they would like to schedule the next turnover.

The report is as important as the clean. Hosts remember the cleaner who told them about the leaky tap before a guest complained.

Contracts and terms that protect you

Short-term rental cleaning has risks that regular house cleaning does not, and verbal agreements are asking for trouble. We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice, so have an attorney in your state review your template. These are the clauses to discuss.

Scope and standards

  • Exactly what each turnover includes, and what is extra
  • The checklist and photo requirements
  • Turnaround windows and what happens if a guest checks out late

Pricing and payment

  • The price per turnover or the retainer amount
  • Surcharges for same-day, holiday, heavy-condition or extra-large clean-ups
  • Payment terms: card on file, weekly invoicing or monthly invoicing
  • Late payment fees and what happens if invoices go unpaid

Scheduling and cancellations

  • How the host shares the calendar (a synced calendar link works well)
  • Minimum notice for cancellations and what fee applies for short-notice cancellations
  • What happens when a guest extends a stay or a reservation changes
  • Your response time for last-minute requests

Access and security

  • How you get access: key lockbox, smart lock code or in-person handoff
  • Who is allowed to enter, and that codes are never shared
  • What happens if access fails

Damage, loss and liability

This is the section that causes the most disputes. Spell out:

  • Your insurance: general liability coverage, bonding if you offer it, and workers' compensation where required
  • How damage found at turnover is reported, and that you document the condition on arrival
  • What you are and are not responsible for, such as accidental damage by your crew versus pre-existing damage or guest damage
  • A reasonable cap on liability, if your attorney advises it
  • How claims are made and resolved

Document everything with photos. The habit of taking arrival and departure photos protects you as much as it reassures the host.

Staffing and compliance

  • That your cleaners are employees or properly classified contractors under your state's rules
  • That workers are background checked where you promise it
  • Any required local business licenses or short-term rental related permits, which vary by city

Term and termination

  • A trial period, then a month-to-month or fixed term
  • Notice required to end the agreement
  • What happens to supplies, keys and codes at termination

Our guide to writing proposals and contracts for commercial accounts has more on structure and wording that also applies to property managers.

Operations: how to deliver reliably at scale

Winning the contract is half the work. Keeping it depends on whether your operation can handle the pace.

Scheduling

Sync host calendars where possible. Many hosts and managers use calendar links or tools that block dates and show checkouts. Build a daily schedule of turnovers, grouped by neighborhood to cut drive time. Always schedule a buffer for late checkouts.

Staffing

Short-term rental work spikes on weekends and holidays. Build a pool of trained cleaners you can call in. Cross-train so no single person is the only one who knows a unit. Keep a written profile for each property: door codes, quirks, linen counts, where supplies live, and photos of how it should look when finished.

Laundry

Decide your model:

  • On site. The cleaner starts a load on arrival and folds on departure. It adds time but avoids transport.
  • Off site. You collect, wash and return linens. It needs storage, transport and extra sets of linens.
  • Linen service. A commercial linen company drops off and picks up. It costs more but removes the labor.

Whichever you pick, build the cost into your price.

Quality control

  • Use a checklist app or a printed list with sign-offs
  • Require after photos of every room
  • Have a supervisor spot-check at random and review photos daily
  • Track callbacks and complaints by cleaner and property

Communication

Pick one channel per client, such as a shared group message, and respond fast. Send a short message when you start and when you finish. Report issues straight away.

Technology

You do not need fancy software to start, but a booking and scheduling tool saves hours once you pass a handful of units. Our booking system service helps cleaning companies take requests, quotes and recurring schedules online so you are not chasing details by text.

Common mistakes cleaning companies make with short-term rentals

  • Underpricing the first clients. Low prices attract price shoppers and burn your cash flow. Charge fairly from day one.
  • Ignoring laundry cost and time. It is often the hidden reason a turnover loses money.
  • No written agreement. Disputes about damage, cancellations and payment are far harder without a contract.
  • Skipping photos. Without them you cannot protect yourself or prove value.
  • Overpromising speed. A four-hour window is not four hours of cleaning. Plan travel and buffers.
  • Taking on too many units too fast. Ramp up with staffing, or quality drops and reviews follow.
  • Using one cleaner for everything. When that person is sick, everything stops.
  • Not asking for referrals. Hosts talk to each other. A happy host is your best salesperson.
  • No dedicated web page. If hosts cannot find you, they will call someone else.

A 60 day launch plan for short-term rental cleaning

Days 1 to 10: Prepare

  • Calculate your real cost per turnover for studio, 1, 2 and 3 bedroom units
  • Write your checklist and photo process
  • Choose your laundry model
  • Check your insurance and update it if needed
  • Draft your service agreement and have an attorney review it
  • Prepare a one-page overview with insurance details and a sample report

Days 11 to 25: Build your presence

  • Add a short-term rental page to your website, with a quote form
  • Update your Google Business Profile with the service and recent photos
  • Ask two or three existing clients who host rentals for a review
  • Join two local host groups and start answering questions helpfully

Days 26 to 45: Reach out

  • Contact ten property managers with a personalized email and a phone follow-up
  • Message twenty individual hosts whose listings show cleanliness complaints, with respect
  • Reach out to five real estate agents who work with investors
  • Offer one trial turnover per prospect

Days 46 to 60: Convert and systemize

  • Send the report and a clear next step after every trial
  • Sign the first contracts and set recurring schedules
  • Ask each new client for a referral
  • Review your numbers: price per turnover, time on site, margin and callbacks

By day 60, you should know whether your price is right, whether your operation can handle the volume and which channel brings the best clients. Adjust and repeat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my competitor rank higher than me on Google?

Usually they have a stronger Google Business Profile, a separate page for each service and area, more recent reviews, and more local websites linking to them. Their website being nicer is rarely the reason.

Can I pay Google to rank higher in the map results?

No. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. You can run ads, but they appear separately and are marked as ads.

How do I get ChatGPT to recommend my cleaning company?

There is no guaranteed method. The basics that help on Google tend to help here too: a complete Google profile, steady reviews, consistent details across the web and a helpful website with a page for each service and area.

Do I need special code or an llms.txt file for AI search?

Google says you don't need special markup or an llms.txt file to appear in its AI features. Focus on helpful pages and strong basics first.

How long will it take to overtake a competitor?

It depends on how far ahead they are and what is holding you back. Anyone who promises a date is guessing. A realistic goal is to fix the basics in 30 days, then keep improving each month.

Does distance matter, and can I change it?

Yes, Google lists distance as one of three factors. You can't move closer to customers, but you can improve relevance and prominence, which is where most of the gains are.
